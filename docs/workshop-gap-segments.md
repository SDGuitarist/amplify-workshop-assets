# Workshop Gap Segments — Teaching Behind Every Concept

**Date:** March 16, 2026
**Purpose:** Fill gaps where the workshop arc mentions a concept but doesn't teach a method. Each segment below is a self-contained teaching moment with setup, demo, hands-on component, and connection to the workshop themes.

**Time budget:** ~18 minutes total (11 min P1/P2 + 7 min P3). Reclaimed by trimming Pipeline Architecture (10 min → 7 min), Naming Dual Literacy (10 min → 7 min), and tightening transitions.

## Segment 1: The 30-Second Rule (Accuracy Verification)

**Fills:** Gap 1 — Evaluation Question #1 ("Is it accurate enough?") has no teaching behind it.
**Priority:** P1
**Where it fits:** Phase 5, immediately before the Five Evaluation Questions (12:46, after Permission Check-In)
**Duration:** 3 minutes

### Why This Matters

AI confidently invents facts. Not sometimes. Regularly. Participants who are new to AI have no instinct for this. They'll read a polished paragraph, assume it's correct because it sounds authoritative, and send it to a client with fabricated details. This is the highest real-world risk in the workshop.

### The Setup (30 sec)

> "Before we evaluate your outputs, I need to show you something that will save you from a very specific kind of embarrassment."

### The Demo (1.5 min)

Project a lead response on screen that Alex generated beforehand. It should look polished, professional, and confident. But it contains three hallucinations:

1. A venue name that doesn't exist ("The Terrace at Harbor View")
2. A pricing detail Alex never provided ("Starting at $1,200 for a 3-hour set")
3. A credential that's slightly wrong ("With over 25 years of experience performing at Balboa Park events" when the real number is different or the venue is wrong)

> "This looks great. Professional. Confident. Ready to send. Except the venue name is made up. I've never quoted that price in my life. And the experience detail is wrong. AI invented all three because I didn't provide them. It filled the gaps with plausible fiction."

Let that land.

> "AI doesn't leave blanks. It doesn't say 'I don't know this detail.' It guesses. And it guesses with the same confidence it uses when it's right. You cannot tell the difference by reading. You can only tell by checking."

### The Rule (30 sec)

> "Here's the rule I use for everything my system produces. I call it the 30-Second Rule."

> "If AI states a fact that you didn't explicitly provide, and you can't verify it in 30 seconds, delete it. Don't rewrite it. Don't soften it. Delete it. A response with a gap is fixable. A response with a confident lie damages your reputation."

> "Venue names. Dates. Prices. Credentials. Client details. Availability. If you didn't feed it, don't trust it."

### The Quick Check (30 sec)

> "Right now, look at the output you generated in the last exercise. Find one fact. Just one. Did you provide it, or did AI generate it? If AI generated it, can you verify it in 30 seconds? If not, highlight it. That's your first edit."

Give them 20 seconds to scan. Don't belabor it.

### Connection to Workshop Themes

- **Dual Literacy:** AI fluency means knowing what AI does well (drafting) and what it does badly (facts). Human literacy means knowing which facts matter in your domain. Both required.
- **The 5 I's:** This is **Integrity** in action. "You can stand behind everything this system produces because you stayed in the loop."
- **Expert-First Prompting:** The more domain-specific facts you put INTO the prompt, the fewer gaps AI fills with fiction. Accuracy is upstream, not downstream.

### Handout Addition

**The 30-Second Rule:**
If AI states a fact you didn't provide, verify it in 30 seconds or delete it. AI doesn't leave blanks. It guesses with confidence. Check: venue names, dates, prices, credentials, client details, availability.

## Segment 2: The Human Signal (What Would You Say That AI Wouldn't Know?)

**Fills:** Gap 8 — The most important facilitation question is buried in a circulating note instead of taught to the whole room.
**Priority:** P1
**Where it fits:** Phase 5, before participants start "Your Lead, Your Expertise" exercise (12:30, as a pre-exercise instruction)
**Duration:** 2 minutes

### Why This Matters

This question IS the core human literacy technique. It's the exact mechanism by which a participant moves from "AI wrote this for me" to "AI wrote this AS me." Currently Alex asks it while circulating, which means only 5-8 participants hear it. The other 22 miss the single most transferable skill in the workshop.

### The Setup (30 sec)

> "Before you start, I want to give you one technique that changes everything about how your output reads. This is the difference between AI content and YOUR content."

### The Technique (1 min)

> "For every AI draft you generate, ask yourself one question: What's the one thing I would say here that AI would never know to include?"

Pause. Let them think.

> "A client's nickname. The fact that parking at that venue is a nightmare and you should mention the side entrance. The inside knowledge that this type of event always starts 20 minutes late, so you should quote setup time differently. The detail that this particular client cares more about atmosphere than song selection because you've worked with them before."

> "AI can't know these things. They're not on the internet. They're not in any training data. They live in YOUR experience. And they're the reason a client reads your response and thinks 'this person gets it.'"

### The Instruction (30 sec)

> "So here's what I want you to do. After AI gives you a draft, read it once. Then add one thing from your own experience that AI couldn't have included. One sentence. One detail. One thing that only you know. That's your human signal. It's what makes the output yours and not just AI's."

> "Ready? Go."

Participants begin the exercise. Alex still circulates and asks the question individually, but now the whole room has the framework.

### Connection to Workshop Themes

- **Dual Literacy:** This IS the human literacy half in one actionable technique. AI provides fluency (the draft). You provide literacy (the human signal).
- **The 5 I's:** This is **Identity** — "your standards, your point of view" — made concrete.
- **Floor and Ceiling:** The human signal is what puts you above the floor. Without it, you're interchangeable with every other AI user.
- **The Slop Hook:** Prompt B in the opening demo worked because it was FULL of human signals (12 years, Latin American families, documentary meets fine art). This technique teaches participants to add those signals after the fact, not just before.

### Handout Addition

**The Human Signal:**
After every AI draft, add one thing from your experience that AI couldn't have known. A client detail. A venue quirk. An industry insight. One sentence. That's what makes the output yours.

## Segment 3: The Tracker Practice Round

**Fills:** Gap 6 — The 14-Day Tracker is introduced in the last 10 minutes with no practice, so participants will never use it.
**Priority:** P2
**Where it fits:** Phase 7 (CLOSE), immediately after introducing the tracker concept (1:52, within the Commit + Take-Home block)
**Duration:** 3 minutes

### Why This Matters

The 14-day tracker is the workshop's only tool for compounding learning after April 25. Without it, participants had a great afternoon and forgot everything by Wednesday. But tools introduced without practice have a near-zero adoption rate. One practice round in the room takes the tracker from "homework I'll never do" to "something I've already started."

### The Setup (30 sec)

After Alex introduces the tracker and explains the three columns:

> "We're going to do round one right now. Not later. Not Monday. Right now. Pull up the last output you generated today."

### The Practice (2 min)

> "Column 1. What did you prompt? Don't rewrite it. Just note the gist. 'Expert-First prompt for a wedding inquiry' or 'Client follow-up with my pricing loaded.' Ten words max. Write it."

Give them 20 seconds.

> "Column 2. What came back? Not the whole output. The verdict. Did it work? Was it close? Was it way off? One sentence. 'Good tone, wrong pricing, too formal at the end.' Write it."

Give them 20 seconds.

> "Column 3. This is the one that matters. What would you change next time? Not in the output. In the PROMPT. Maybe you'd add your actual pricing. Maybe you'd include a sample of how you close emails. Maybe you'd tell AI to drop the formality. One specific change. Write it."

Give them 30 seconds.

### The Close (30 sec)

> "That's round one. You just completed 20% of your homework. Four more to go. And here's what happens if you actually do all five: by round three, you'll stop needing to think about what to change. Your prompts will start reflecting your expertise automatically. That's the compound effect. Each round makes the next one better."

> "That third column is where the learning lives. Prompt, output, adjustment. Prompt, output, adjustment. Five rounds of that and the system isn't mine anymore. It's yours."

### Connection to Workshop Themes

- **Dual Literacy:** The tracker IS the dual literacy training program. Column 1 is AI fluency (what you asked). Column 3 is human literacy (what your expertise tells you to fix).
- **The 5 I's:** This is **Iteration** — "not failure, craft" — made into a repeatable habit.
- **Compound step (from Claude Code lens):** The third column is what turns a one-time tool into a compounding system. Without it, the tracker is just a log.

### Handout Addition

The tracker should come pre-filled with Round 1 headers and a completed example row:

| # | What I Prompted | What Came Back | What I'd Change |
|---|----------------|----------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Expert-First pitch for cocktail hour gig, included my Latin jazz background + venue history | Tone was right, mentioned genres I don't play, closing was too formal | Add my actual repertoire list, include a sample closing from a real email I sent |
| 2 | | | |
| 3 | | | |
| 4 | | | |
| 5 | | | |

## Segment 4: How to Give Feedback on AI Output

**Fills:** Gap 7 — The cross-category pair exercise (Phase 5, 12:48-1:00) asks participants to give feedback but never teaches them what useful feedback looks like.
**Priority:** P2
**Where it fits:** Phase 5, immediately before the pair swap (12:55, within the Five Evaluation Questions block)
**Duration:** 1 minute

### Why This Matters

Without this, the pair exercise becomes polite nodding. "Looks good!" doesn't help anyone. The whole point of cross-category pairs is that a photographer can spot voice problems in a consultant's draft because they're reading as a client, not a colleague. But only if they know what to look for.

### The Instruction (1 min)

After explaining the Five Evaluation Questions and before pairs swap drafts:

> "In a minute you're going to swap drafts with your partner and give one piece of feedback. Here's how to make that feedback actually useful."

> "Don't say 'this is good.' Don't say 'I like it.' That tells your partner nothing. Instead, do two things."

> "First: point to the one line that sounds most like a real person wrote it. The line where you can hear their voice. Tell them which line and why."

> "Second: point to the one line that sounds most like AI wrote it. The line where the voice disappears. Maybe it's a word they'd never use. Maybe it's a sentence that could have come from anyone. Maybe it's just too smooth. Point to the exact words."

> "One line that sounds like them. One line that sounds like a robot. That's the feedback. Specific, not polite."

### Example (optional, if time allows, 30 sec)

Project a sample draft and point:

> "For example: 'I've been doing this for 15 years and the cocktail hour is where most vendors phone it in' — that sounds like a person with an opinion. 'I would be delighted to discuss how we can create a truly memorable experience for your guests' — that sounds like AI. See the difference? One has a point of view. The other could have come from anywhere."

### Connection to Workshop Themes

- **Dual Literacy:** The pair exercise is peer-to-peer human literacy training. You're using another person's judgment as a mirror for your own voice.
- **The 5 I's:** This serves **Identity** (detecting where voice is present vs. absent) and **Iteration** (the feedback becomes the next prompt revision).
- **The Slop Hook:** The opening demo taught them to see the gap between flat and expert-led. This teaches them to see the gap in their OWN output.

### Handout Addition

**Feedback Template:**
When reviewing someone's AI output, find: (1) the one line that sounds most like them, and (2) the one line that sounds most like AI. Point to the exact words.

## Segment 5: The Manual Email Test (Value vs. Quality Drop)

**Fills:** Gap 3 — Evaluation Question #5 ("Does it add value — or does it just save time while lowering quality?") has no method for measuring quality change.
**Priority:** P2
**Where it fits:** Phase 5, as part of the Five Evaluation Questions explanation (12:49, when Alex introduces Question #5)
**Duration:** 2 minutes

### Why This Matters

This is the question that separates thoughtful AI users from slop producers. But "does it add value?" is too abstract for a room of first-time AI users. They need a concrete comparison method. Without one, everyone answers "yes" because the AI output exists and they didn't have to write it from scratch. That's not value. That's convenience mistaken for quality.

### The Setup (30 sec)

When introducing Evaluation Question #5:

> "This is the hardest question on the list. And it's the one most people skip because the answer feels obvious. 'Of course it adds value. It saved me 20 minutes.' But saving time and adding value are not the same thing. Sometimes AI saves you 20 minutes and costs you a client because the response was generic."

### The Test (1 min)

> "Here's how you actually answer this question. I call it the Manual Email Test."

> "Think of the last real email you sent to a client. Not a form response. A real one where you were being yourself and doing your best work. Maybe it took you 15 minutes. Maybe you agonized over a sentence. Picture it."

> "Now read your AI draft. If your client got both messages, side by side, which one would make them feel like they're working with a real person who understands their situation? Which one would make them want to hire you?"

> "If the manual email wins, the AI draft isn't adding value. It's just saving time while making you sound worse. And that's a bad trade."

Pause.

> "If the AI draft wins, or even ties, you've done something right. Your expertise went into the prompt, and the output reflects it. That's the version worth sending."

### The Bridge (30 sec)

> "The goal isn't 'use AI for everything.' The goal is 'use AI for things where it matches or beats your best manual work.' Anything below that bar, you're trading your reputation for convenience. Question 5 is how you check."

> "So right now, look at your draft. Does it beat your best manual email? Or just your laziest one? If it only beats lazy, go back and improve the prompt."

### Connection to Workshop Themes

- **Dual Literacy:** AI fluency tells you what AI CAN produce. Human literacy tells you whether that's GOOD ENOUGH. This test is the bridge.
- **The Wedge:** "Work deeper, not faster." If AI makes you faster but shallower, you've gone the wrong direction. This test catches that.
- **Floor and Ceiling:** The manual email is your ceiling. If AI output is below it, you dropped toward the floor. The goal is AI output that matches your ceiling, freeing you to raise it.
- **The 5 I's:** This is **Integrity** — ensuring the output meets a standard you'd stake your reputation on.

### Handout Addition

**The Manual Email Test:**
Compare your AI draft to the last real email you sent a client. If the manual email makes the client feel more understood, the AI draft isn't adding value. Fix the prompt until the AI version matches or beats your best manual work.

## Segment 6: The Client Camera Test (Emotional Credibility)

**Fills:** Gap 2 — Evaluation Question #4 ("Is it emotionally credible?") is the most abstract question with no rubric or method.
**Priority:** P3
**Where it fits:** Phase 5, within the Five Evaluation Questions explanation (12:50, when Alex introduces Question #4)
**Duration:** 2 minutes

### Why This Matters

"Emotionally credible" means nothing to a first-time AI user. They'll read the question, shrug, and skip it. But this is actually the question that catches the deepest AI failure mode: output that's technically correct and sounds professional but feels hollow. The client reads it and thinks "this person doesn't actually care about my event." That's not an accuracy problem or a voice problem. It's an emotional credibility problem. And it's the fastest way to lose a booking.

### The Setup (30 sec)

When introducing Question #4:

> "This next question sounds fuzzy but it's the most important one for anyone who works with clients face to face. 'Is it emotionally credible?' Here's what that means in plain English."

### The Test (1 min)

> "Imagine your client is standing in the room right now, reading your AI draft over your shoulder. Not a stranger. YOUR client. The one who emailed you about their mom's 70th birthday party, or their company retreat, or their wedding."

> "As they read it, watch their face in your mind. Are they nodding? Do they feel seen? Or is there a moment where their expression shifts and you think 'I'd never actually say that to them in person'?"

> "That moment. That flinch. That's emotional credibility failing."

Give a concrete example:

> "Here's what it looks like. AI writes: 'I would be honored to be part of your special day and help create lasting memories for you and your guests.' That's not wrong. It's not inaccurate. But if you said that to a bride's face at a coffee meeting, you'd sound like a greeting card. You'd say something like: 'Tell me about the vibe you're going for. Is this more of a party or more of a moment?' That's emotionally credible. It shows you're actually listening, not performing."

### The Rule (30 sec)

> "The test is simple. I call it the Client Camera. Imagine your client is watching you read the draft to them. Any line that would make you cringe, or pause, or want to skip over. That's where emotional credibility breaks. Rewrite those lines in the words you'd actually use if the client were sitting across the table."

> "Look at your draft now. Find the one line you'd skip if you were reading it aloud to your client. That's your edit."

### Connection to Workshop Themes

- **Dual Literacy:** AI fluency produces polished text. Human literacy knows whether that polish feels real or performed. The Client Camera tests the human literacy half.
- **The 5 I's:** This is **Identity** — "your standards, your point of view" — applied to emotional register, not just vocabulary.
- **Voice Training connection:** The voice training segment teaches participants to catch words that don't sound like them. The Client Camera catches tone that doesn't feel like them. Words and tone are different failure modes.
- **The Slop Hook:** The flat prompt in the opening demo failed emotionally before it failed technically. "Truly special" and "memorable experience" are emotionally incredible because they're what everyone says. The expert-led version worked because it contained specifics that proved understanding.

### Handout Addition

**The Client Camera:**
Imagine your client reading the draft over your shoulder. Any line that makes you want to skip or rephrase. Rewrite it in the words you'd actually use across a table.

## Segment 7: The Trust Ladder (Permission Decision Rule)

**Fills:** Gap 4 — The Permission Check-In (Phase 5, 12:45) validates the feeling of unease but gives no framework for WHEN to trust AI output and when not to.
**Priority:** P3
**Where it fits:** Phase 5, extending the Permission Check-In (12:47, immediately after the current validation language)
**Duration:** 2.5 minutes

### Why This Matters

"You're still the one deciding" is reassuring but not actionable. Participants leave the Permission Check-In feeling better about their discomfort but with no decision rule for their actual work. Monday morning, a lead comes in. AI drafts a response. The participant stares at it and thinks: "Is this good enough to send? Am I cheating? Should I rewrite the whole thing myself?" Without a framework, they either send everything (overcorrecting toward efficiency) or rewrite everything (overcorrecting toward authenticity) and eventually stop using AI altogether. A decision rule prevents both failure modes.

### The Setup (30 sec)

After the current Permission Check-In language ("That discomfort? That's your standards talking."):

> "So the question becomes: when DO you trust it? Not a feeling. A rule. Here's the one I use."

### The Trust Ladder (1.5 min)

> "I think of AI output on a ladder with four rungs. Each rung has a different trust level and a different action."

Project or describe:

> "**Rung 1: Send as-is.** The output nails your voice, the facts are verified, and you'd put your name on it right now. This is rare with a new system. It gets more common as you train it. Action: send it."

> "**Rung 2: Edit and send.** The structure is right, the intent is right, but a few lines need your touch. A word swap here, a detail added there. This is where most good AI output lives. Action: spend 2-3 minutes editing, then send."

> "**Rung 3: Use as a starting point.** The draft gives you a direction but it's not close to sendable. Maybe the approach is wrong, or the tone is off, or it's missing critical context. Action: keep the parts that work, rewrite the rest. This is still faster than starting from scratch."

> "**Rung 4: Throw it away.** The output is worse than what you'd write yourself. It's misleading, off-tone, or just generic filler. This happens when the prompt didn't carry enough of your expertise. Action: don't try to fix it. Go back to the prompt and start over. Or write it yourself. Sometimes that's the right call."

### The Principle (30 sec)

> "Here's the important part. There's no shame in any rung. Rung 4 isn't failure. Rung 1 isn't the goal every time. The skill is recognizing which rung you're on and acting accordingly. The people who burn out on AI are the ones who try to force Rung 4 output into Rung 2 by editing forever. The people who get burned by AI are the ones who treat Rung 3 like Rung 1 and send it without editing."

> "Know which rung. Act accordingly. That's the decision rule."

### Connection to Workshop Themes

- **Dual Literacy:** The Trust Ladder requires both literacies. AI fluency to evaluate the output quality. Human literacy to know what "good enough" means in your domain. A photographer's Rung 1 is different from a consultant's Rung 1.
- **The 5 I's:** This is **Iteration** made into a decision framework. "Not failure. Craft." The ladder normalizes every outcome as part of the process.
- **Permission Check-In integration:** The validation ("your standards are talking") now has a concrete follow-up ("here's how to listen to those standards and act on them").
- **The 14-Day Tracker:** Column 3 ("what would you change?") is much easier to fill in when participants can name the rung. "It was Rung 3. The approach was right but the tone was off. Next time I'll include a sample email for tone reference."
- **Floor and Ceiling:** Rung 1 is the ceiling. Rung 4 is below the floor. The ladder helps participants stay above the floor without expecting to hit the ceiling every time.

### Handout Addition

**The Trust Ladder:**

| Rung | What It Looks Like | Action |
|------|-------------------|--------|
| 1. Send as-is | Nails your voice, facts verified, you'd sign it | Send |
| 2. Edit and send | Structure right, needs your touch | 2-3 min edit, send |
| 3. Starting point | Direction is right, not sendable yet | Keep what works, rewrite the rest |
| 4. Throw it away | Worse than what you'd write yourself | Fix the prompt or write it manually |

No shame in any rung. The skill is knowing which one you're on.

## Segment 8: The Stopwatch Demo (Capacity Reframe Experience)

**Fills:** Gap 5 — The Capacity Reframe (Phase 1, 10:19) is stated but never demonstrated. Participants hear "the problem is capacity" but don't feel it.
**Priority:** P3
**Where it fits:** Phase 1, replacing or extending the Capacity Reframe (10:19-10:24). Can also be compressed into the Expert-First Exercise (Phase 3) as a timed comparison.
**Duration:** 2.5 minutes

### Why This Matters

"The problem is capacity, not knowledge" is the most important reframe in the workshop. It's the line that shifts participants from "I need to learn AI" to "I need to deploy what I already know." But right now it's a statement. Participants nod. They don't feel it in their gut. The best way to make someone feel a capacity problem is to show them the gap between what they know and what they can actually execute in real time.

### Option A: Phase 1 Version (Storytelling, No Laptops)

This version works in Phase 1 where laptops aren't open yet. It's a thought experiment, not a hands-on exercise.

**The Setup (30 sec)**

After the current Capacity Reframe language ("You can't deploy what you know consistently"):

> "Let me make this concrete. I want you to do some quick math with me."

**The Math (1.5 min)**

> "Think about the last time you got an inquiry from a potential client. How long did it take you to write a really good response? Not a quick 'thanks for reaching out.' A response where you qualified the lead, addressed their specific situation, referenced your experience, maybe suggested a package or a price, and closed with a clear next step. Your A-game response."

> "For most people in this room, that's 15 to 20 minutes. Maybe longer if you're checking your calendar, looking up past gig details, or just trying to find the right words."

> "Now. How many leads do you get in a week? Two? Five? Ten? Multiply that by 15 minutes. A photographer getting five inquiries a week is spending over an hour just on first responses. Not shooting. Not editing. Not meeting clients. Just responding."

> "And here's the part nobody talks about. That hour and fifteen minutes is only if you respond immediately. What actually happens? You see the lead. You're in the middle of something. You tell yourself you'll get to it later. Three hours pass. Six hours. A day. By the time you respond, half the magic is gone. The client already contacted someone else. Not because you didn't know what to say. Because you didn't have the capacity to say it when it mattered."

**The Bridge (30 sec)**

> "That's the problem we're solving today. Not teaching you what to say. You already know what to say. Giving you the capacity to say it every time, on time, at your standard. That's what the systems I built do. That's what you're going to learn to build."

### Option B: Phase 3 Version (Timed Hands-On)

This version works in Phase 3 where laptops are open. It replaces the first round of the Expert-First exercise with a timed manual attempt.

**The Setup (30 sec)**

Before the Expert-First Exercise:

> "Before we use AI, I want you to feel something first."

**The Timed Write (1.5 min)**

> "Here's a sample lead. [Project a realistic inquiry on screen.] You have 90 seconds to write the best response you can. Manually. No AI. Go."

Start a visible timer. Let the room write. At 90 seconds, call time.

> "Stop. Read what you wrote. Is that your A-game? Or is that what you produce under time pressure?"

Pause. Most will have something incomplete or rushed.

> "Now imagine that's not one lead. It's three. At the same time. On a Tuesday afternoon when you're also prepping for a gig, answering a venue's email, and trying to eat lunch."

> "That's the capacity problem. You KNOW what a great response looks like. You just don't have the time and mental bandwidth to produce it consistently. That's what we're about to fix."

Then proceed directly into the Expert-First Exercise. The contrast between their rushed manual attempt and the AI-assisted version becomes the most powerful before/after in the workshop.

**The Bridge (30 sec)**

> "Keep that 90-second version. We're going to come back to it. By the end of the next exercise, you'll have something better, written in a fraction of the time. The difference won't be that AI is smarter than you. The difference will be that AI gave you the capacity to deploy what you already know."

### Recommendation

**Use Option B if Phase 3 timing allows.** The timed hands-on version is dramatically more effective because participants feel the capacity gap in their body (rushed writing, incomplete thoughts, time pressure anxiety) rather than just hearing about it. It also creates the strongest possible contrast with the Expert-First exercise that follows.

**Use Option A if Phase 1 needs more substance or Phase 3 is too tight.** The math version works as a narrative amplifier after the Capacity Reframe statement. It's less powerful than the timed version but requires no restructuring.

**The ideal implementation uses both:** Option A as narrative setup in Phase 1 (the math), Option B as experiential proof in Phase 3 (the stopwatch). The math plants the seed. The stopwatch makes them feel it. The Expert-First exercise solves it. Three-beat emotional arc.

### Connection to Workshop Themes

- **Dual Literacy:** The capacity gap is WHY you need AI fluency. Without it, your human literacy (your expertise, your voice, your judgment) gets bottlenecked by operational load. AI fluency removes the bottleneck.
- **The Wedge:** "Work deeper, not faster" becomes visceral. The 90-second attempt shows what "faster" without depth looks like. The Expert-First output shows what "deeper" looks like.
- **AI Anxiety → AI Agency:** The capacity gap is the hidden driver of AI anxiety. Participants aren't really afraid AI will replace them. They're afraid they can't keep up. Showing that AI solves the keeping-up problem (capacity) without touching the quality problem (expertise) is the fastest path from anxiety to agency.
- **The Three Questions connection:** Question 2 ("Where are you just doing tasks?") is about capacity. The stopwatch demo makes that answer visceral before they ever write it down.
- **The 5 I's:** This segment grounds **Intent** — "the purpose and vision you bring" — by showing that intent without capacity produces rushed, inferior work. AI restores the capacity to execute your intent fully.

### Handout Addition

**The Capacity Check:**
Time yourself writing one client response manually. Multiply by your weekly inquiries. That's the time AI gives back. The goal isn't replacing your expertise. It's giving you the capacity to deploy it consistently.
